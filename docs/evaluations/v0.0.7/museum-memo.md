# Operational recommendation

## Decision and evidence

Recommend a reversible pilot combining clear destination signs with a brief welcome at the existing entrance. Keep one shared arrival point and the existing ticketing process. The intended beneficiaries are visitors uncertain about their next step and the two staff managing arrival. Prefer the combined approach only while essential duties remain covered and observations suggest an advantage over signage alone.

Supplied facts: the fictional museum has one entrance and two arrival staff; visitors report confusion between prebooked entry, walk-in ticketing and accessibility assistance. No queue counts, service times, budget or conversion data are supplied. The source explicitly states: “Do not invent measured gains or require a new app.” This recommendation makes no measured improvement claim and requires no app.

Causal hypothesis: visible instructions resolve routine uncertainty while a short human interaction resolves exceptions before visitors reach the wrong service point. This is a reason to test the approach, not evidence that it works.

## Physical setup and staff responsibilities

Use movable signs and existing materials where available. Place the first sign where visitors can see it before choosing their next step, outside circulation and emergency access paths. Suggested wording: “Already booked? Have your booking ready for Entry checks. Need a ticket? Go to Tickets. Need help with entry or access? Ask either member of staff.” Match those labels to existing service locations; add directional arrows only after checking the actual layout.

Staff 1 retains existing entrance and checking duties and offers brief orientation when capacity permits: “Welcome. Do you already have a booking, or do you need a ticket? I can also help with entry or access.” Visitors who know where to go should proceed without a compulsory greeting stop. Staff 2 handles walk-in ticketing through the existing process. Both provide assistance within their competence and existing remit.

Essential duties take priority. If checking, a transaction or assistance consumes available capacity, suspend proactive greeting and use the signs with existing service arrangements. If this happens routinely, the combined approach is probably unsuitable with two staff; signage alone becomes the preferred operating option.

Before implementation, the museum’s responsible staff should confirm sign positions, materials, actual incremental costs, an acceptable spending limit and authority for the proposed duties. Conduct a tabletop walkthrough of simultaneous prebooked arrival, a walk-in transaction and an assistance request. These are proposed checks, not completed validation.

## Accessibility within the shared journey

Offer assistance throughout the same arrival journey, without a separate accessibility queue or a requirement to explain a disability publicly. Ask what would help and respect the visitor’s communication preference. Offer a discreet conversation where practical without leaving essential duties uncovered, and use existing assistance arrangements when needed.

Use plain language, readable high-contrast text and clear visual cues; do not rely on colour alone. Personal guidance must remain available to visitors who cannot perceive or understand the signs. Check sightlines, reach and unobstructed movement before placement, and invite voluntary feedback about clarity and access. A shared entrance alone does not establish accessibility; the proposed arrangement remains unvalidated.

## Comparison with alternatives

Signage only: use the same signs at the entrance while staff continue their existing duties and assistance. This is simple, reversible and consumes little ongoing staff attention. Its limitation is that static information cannot reliably resolve unusual ticket situations or every communication need. Retain it as the explicit comparator and as the fallback whenever greeting compromises coverage.

Signs plus greeting: adds an adaptable human route to clarification, with the potential to prevent misdirection. Its additional cost is staff attention. The incremental benefit must therefore be observable without deterioration in access or essential-duty coverage.

Verbal orientation alone: requires no new equipment and permits tailored explanations, but repeats routine information and leaves visitors without guidance when both staff are occupied. Use a consistent script within the combined pilot rather than relying on speech alone.

## Prospective pilot and observation record

Proposed trial duration, not a source fact: start with two comparable opening days, observing one quieter and one busier 30-minute window on each day. Use signage only in the quieter window and signs plus greeting in the busier window on the first day; reverse the allocation on the second. This gives each condition exposure to both demand contexts while limiting the initial recording burden. Match clock times where practical, keep sign wording unchanged and record differences in visitor mix, group arrivals, staffing and crowding. Two days provide an initial operational comparison, not a sufficient sample by definition.

Use a simple paper tally only if it fits around essential duties; assume no additional observer. Record arriving visitors as the denominator. Misdirection means a visitor reaches the wrong service point and needs redirection. Repeat clarification means a visitor asks again after initial guidance. Count each visitor at most once within each measure per window; a visitor can appear in both measures, so do not add them together. An initial request for assistance is not itself confusion.

Blank observation template: Date ___; start/end ___; condition ___; quieter/busier context ___; arriving visitors ___; visitors misdirected ___; visitors seeking repeat clarification ___; access barriers or voluntary feedback ___; essential-duty gaps ___; crowding or blocked passage ___; greeting suspensions ___; observation gaps ___; contextual differences ___; action taken ___.

For fully observed windows, report misdirected visitors divided by arriving visitors and repeat-clarification visitors divided by arriving visitors, alongside the underlying counts. Leave rates undefined when no arrivals are observed. Mark incomplete observation as missing, never zero, and do not use a full-window denominator for partially observed events. Service takes priority over recording; if reliable observation is impractical, the effectiveness finding remains inconclusive.

## Success, stop criteria and next decision

Provisional success requires lower observed misdirection and repeat-clarification rates in the combined condition across comparable quieter and busier windows, with no observed deterioration in access or essential-duty coverage. Consider visitor feedback and greeting suspensions alongside the rates. This is a proposed decision rule, not a measured result or a claim of statistical significance. Zero confusion in both conditions would show no demonstrated incremental benefit.

Either staff member can call an immediate pause for obstructed passage or emergency access, an access need the arrangement cannot accommodate, an uncovered essential duty, or crowding staff cannot safely manage. Suspend greeting, remove or reposition movable pilot items as needed, and return to the existing safe arrangement while addressing the visitor’s immediate need through existing assistance procedures. Keep signs only where their placement remains safe.

After the trial, retain signage alone if greeting adds no observable benefit or repeatedly disrupts coverage. If evidence is favourable, consider a further limited trial before permanent adoption. Mixed results, sparse arrivals, missing records or materially different conditions remain inconclusive; they do not validate the combined approach. No shorter-queue or throughput conclusion is supported by this protocol.
